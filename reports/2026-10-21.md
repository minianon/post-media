Your AI feature will hit a rate limit. Plan for it before it ships.

This is something I had to handle in AutoPress, my AI news site. It writes long reports with Groq's free tier, and free tiers say "slow down" often.

A fixed sleep and retry is the obvious fix, but it is the wrong shape. Too short and you hammer the API. Too long and the job crawls.

What the retry code in AutoPress does:
- If the error message says "try again in 1m5s", parse it and wait exactly that, plus a 2 second buffer
- Else, if there is a retry-after header, use that
- Else, exponential backoff with jitter, capped at 30 seconds
- Give up after 4 attempts

The lesson: when the server tells you how long to wait, believe it. Guessing is only the fallback.

Two small design choices make this work better:
- Articles are processed one at a time, so one slow call does not trigger a burst of retries
- Results are saved after each article, so a crash does not throw away finished work

Retries are easy to write and easy to get subtly wrong. Read your error messages. They often contain the answer.

4 questions to ask before you add a cache:

- What is actually slow, and did you measure it?
- How stale can this data be?
- Who invalidates it, and when?
- What happens when the cache is down?

A cache you can't invalidate is just a bug with good latency.

1/5 Cache-aside is the most common caching pattern, and most apps use it without naming it. Explained in 5 tweets 🧵 (115 chars)

2/5 The app owns the logic. On a read it checks the cache first.

Hit? Return it. Miss? Read from the database, then store the result in the cache for next time. (161 chars)

3/5 Writes: update the database, then delete (invalidate) the cached key. The next read repopulates it.

Deleting is usually safer than updating the cache entry in place. (170 chars)

4/5 The costs: the first read of a key is always slow, data can be stale until the TTL expires, and a popular key expiring can send a burst of misses to the database. (166 chars)

5/5 Good fit for read-heavy data that tolerates a little staleness. Poor fit when every read must be perfectly fresh.
