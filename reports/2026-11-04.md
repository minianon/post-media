A second AI pass, told to prove the first one wrong, caught what I missed.

For Buildability, I had an agent research 100 apps: auth method, self-serve or gated access, API surface, official MCP. Every app went through one schema, so the 100 runs were comparable.

The part that made the data trustworthy was the second pass. A verifier agent was prompted to refute the first one, re-search the docs and correct fields.

Then I spot-checked samples against live docs myself. That caught a real miss: Plaid's free Sandbox was labelled a trial. On the conclusive sample, accuracy moved from 82% to 100% after the fixes. Sampled docs I could not reach are marked UNVERIFIABLE, not guessed.

What I took from it:
- Give the checker the opposite job. "Review this" gets agreement. "Refute this" finds bugs.
- Keep the analysis in plain Python. The stats are deterministic, only the research uses an LLM.
- Make runs resumable. The agent fleet hit a session limit, so it ran in three waves and only redid missing apps.

#4 · 6:00 PM · Thread (4 tweets) · Thread · JavaScript · Promise.all vs allSettled
🖼 https://raw.githubusercontent.com/minianon/post-media/main/2026/11-04/x-1200-1.png (tweet 1)

1/4 One agent fails and your whole request dies. Sounds familiar?

In JavaScript this is the gap between Promise.all and Promise.allSettled.  [140 chars]

2/4 Promise.all rejects as soon as one promise rejects. You lose the results of the ones that succeeded.

Fine for all-or-nothing work. Painful for independent tasks.  [166 chars]

3/4 Promise.allSettled waits for every promise and gives you a status for each: fulfilled or rejected.

You keep the good results and handle the failures separately.  [165 chars]

4/4 Rule of thumb: tasks depend on each other, use all. Tasks are independent and partial results are useful, use allSettled.

Which one do you reach for by default?  [165 chars]
